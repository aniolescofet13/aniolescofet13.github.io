About Me

Growing up in Catalonia, I was the kind of kid who genuinely could not sit still. Every tree was something to climb, every rooftop a challenge worth taking. Sports were a constant — anything physical, anything with a bit of edge. That energy never really went away. It just found better places to go.

But alongside all of that, there was always a pull towards physics, maths and understanding how things work. Not as a school obligation, but as genuine curiosity. Outside of that I train in some form every day: gym, running, padel, climbing, hiking. I travel whenever I can. And I keep personal engineering projects running on the side because building things out of pure curiosity is one of the best ways I know to stay sharp.

What I am after is a motorsport or prototype project that genuinely puts me to the test. Something fast-paced, technically demanding, where the engineering work shows up directly in the results. The goal is not a title on a business card. It is the kind of project where you feel the weight of every decision you make.

PP — 01

Lap Time Simulator

Modular Vehicle Development Platform · MATLAB

A MATLAB-based lap time simulation and vehicle analysis platform designed to support a racing car's full development cycle, from early concept where almost no hardware data exists, all the way through to race-ready validation against real on-track telemetry. The tool is built around the principle that the fidelity of the simulation should match the fidelity of the information available: as the team learns more about the car, the models are upgraded in place without restructuring the workflow.

The problem it solves. In motorsport development, the decisions with the biggest impact on performance — suspension geometry, aero package, powertrain architecture — are made at the very beginning of a project, when the least data is available. A tool that only works once full hardware data exists arrives too late. Equally, a tool that is too simplified fails to give useful answers once real data starts coming in. This simulator solves that by being continuously useful at every stage of development, from back-of-envelope concept studies to lap-accurate simulation validated against telemetry.

Three-level fidelity architecture. Every subsystem — Powertrain, Aerodynamics, Brakes, Tyres, Suspension, Chassis — is implemented at three independent levels of physical fidelity, selectable at runtime. L1 (concept stage) uses simple scalar parameters: constant peak power, fixed drag and lift coefficients, constant friction. Enough to produce a credible lap time estimate from a one-page spec sheet. L2 (development stage) introduces physics-based models: torque curves, brake bias and hydraulic geometry, load-sensitive tyre behaviour, lateral load transfer. L3 (validation stage) goes to full fidelity: Pacejka Magic Formula tyres fitted to measured data, full hydraulic brake model with thermal degradation, DRS and ride-height-sensitive aero, ERS energy management. Each upgrade is independent — Tyres L2 can run alongside Suspension L1 if that is all the data available. The simulator loop never knows which level is active: each subsystem exposes a clean interface and higher-fidelity models are drop-in replacements.

Layer 1: QSS spatial sweep. The spatial engine is built on the Quasi-Steady-State method, the industry standard in motorsport simulation. The car is treated as a point mass navigating a known racing line, assumed to be in steady state at every spatial point. The algorithm runs in three linked sweeps across the track distance domain. First, corner speed limits: at every point with curvature κ, the maximum lateral speed is solved iteratively, since downforce and therefore grip depend on speed itself. Second, a forward acceleration sweep propagates the speed envelope accounting for engine power, drag, rolling resistance, and remaining longitudinal grip after cornering demand is subtracted via the friction ellipse. Third, a backward braking sweep runs the same logic in reverse, propagating maximum entry speeds into each corner. Five alternating passes produce the minimum-time speed envelope: at every track point the car is at exactly one limit — traction, braking, or cornering.

Layer 2: transient temporal integration and iterative coupling. Once the spatial speed profile is converged, a second pass integrates the trajectory in the time domain, computing the states that evolve continuously along the lap. The tyre thermal model tracks compound temperature using a lumped thermal network, updating friction coefficients and slip stiffness that feed back into Layer 1. Suspension and chassis dynamics compute instantaneous ride height as a function of heave and roll, which directly modifies the aero maps used in the spatial sweep. ERS state of charge is integrated from the power demand profile, determining deployment availability corner by corner. Fuel mass drops continuously, shifting total weight and CG height. These outputs are fed back into Layer 1, which re-solves the speed profile with the updated vehicle state. The two layers iterate to convergence, typically in three to five passes, producing a speed profile that accounts for both instantaneous physics and the evolving state of the car across the full lap.

Sensitivity analysis and signal explorer. Rather than running the simulator once per configuration, the sensitivity module sweeps a parameter over a range and plots the lap time response directly, answering questions like: at what downforce level does added drag outweigh added cornering grip, what is the weight penalty per second on a technical versus a high-speed circuit, or at what CG height does lateral load transfer start to hurt more than it helps. The signal explorer provides a graphical interface to inspect every computed channel as a function of track position or time — speed, longitudinal and lateral acceleration, tyre load per axle, aerodynamic downforce, brake force distribution, engine operating point, ERS contribution — and overlays channels from different configurations to directly show where one setup gains on another. The GG diagram is included as a standard assessment of how efficiently the tyre friction circle is being used.

Real data validation loop. Once the car is running on track, the simulator's outputs are compared directly against real telemetry — GPS speed, IMU accelerations, wheel speeds, brake pressure, engine channels — aligned spatially by arc length. Systematic offsets in specific sectors reveal which subsystem model needs refinement: a braking point consistently too early points to the brake model, a corner speed consistently too high points to the lateral tyre model. This closes the loop between simulation and reality and gives each model upgrade a clear validation target.

GUI and software architecture. The graphical interface is built entirely in programmatic MATLAB with no external toolboxes. The track tab imports GPS or Cartesian data and displays the racing line coloured by curvature, elevation profile, and corner classification. Each subsystem has its own tab with a level selector that dynamically rebuilds the parameter table and live preview plots — torque curve for Powertrain L2, Pacejka curves at nominal and 2× load for Tyres L3. The results tab shows the speed profile, GG diagram, trajectory coloured by speed, and lap time delta versus a reference. The underlying architecture is object-oriented with clean subsystem interfaces, designed so that upgrading one module never requires touching another — the same principle that makes the tool usable at every stage of a project's life.

The simulator as a web application. The MATLAB engine has been ported to a Python physics core behind a FastAPI service, with a React and three.js front end over it — the same models and the same numbers, reachable from a browser rather than from a MATLAB licence. The garage is where the fidelity architecture stops being a diagram and becomes a control: every subsystem carries its own level selector and reports how many parameters that level asks for, so the cost of an upgrade is on screen before it is taken. A Formula 1 car at L1 across the board reads 456 kW, 317 km/h and 0–100 in 1.46 s. The same car at Chassis L3 (12 parameters), Powertrain L4 with ERS energy management (24), Pacejka tyres at L3 (27), aero with DRS at L3 (10), a hydraulic brake model at L2 (15) and suspension with roll centres at L4 (12) reads 747 kW, 333 km/h and 0–100 in 2.31 s. The detailed model is the slower one, and that is the point: L1 is optimistic because constant power, rigid suspension and constant friction have nothing to lose to.

The G-G-V envelope — longitudinal against lateral capability at every speed — is drawn as a 2D section or as the full 3D surface, with separate states for DRS shut and DRS open. Solving a lap returns the time and the headline numbers beside it: v max, average and minimum, peak braking and lateral g, and the number of shifts. The track map is coloured by speed, the lap’s operating points are scattered onto the G-G-V to show how much of the envelope was actually used, and the speed profile is shaded by which limit binds at each metre — green for traction, blue for cornering, red for braking. Freezing a configuration as a reference and then changing one thing gives the delta directly, which is the loop the whole tool exists to shorten.

Formula Student events need no track. Acceleration over 75 m and the skidpad are built from the rulebook’s own geometry rather than from a circuit file, so a car can be assessed against the events it will actually be scored on: 3.433 s and 4.786 s for the electric car above, with skidpad radius, lateral load and speed at the line beside them. The same panel reads the result back as a diagnosis — no upshift over the whole run, check the final drive — which is the difference between a number and an answer.

Every channel, and the lap driven back. The signal explorer exposes all 47 computed channels — speed, longitudinal and lateral acceleration, combined g, curvature, traction force, available power, gear, engine speed and torque, ERS state of charge, deployment and recovery, downforce front and rear, drag, aero balance, DRS state, normal load — grouped by subsystem and searchable, and any of them can be dropped into one of six stacked rows against distance or against time. One of those channels is the drive zone itself, coded 1 for acceleration, 0 for cornering and −1 for braking, so the classification the solver used is inspectable rather than implied. Dragging on any chart zooms every chart with it, across all 915 stations of the lap.

The replay drives the solved lap back in three dimensions at its own computed pace, from five cameras — chase, onboard, trackside, top and free — with speed, throttle and brake on a HUD, a live g-g dot, and the speed trace as a scrubber underneath. Playback runs from a quarter speed to five times. It is not decoration: a lap time is one number and a speed profile is a curve, but seeing where the car is slow and what the tyres are being asked for at that moment is how a setup decision actually gets made.

The whole thing, in one pass. A recorded walkthrough: the garage, a subsystem raised from L1 to L3, a circuit, a lap solved, the signal explorer, four of the five replay cameras, and the sweep and the validation at the end. Raising the chassis and tyres partway through moves the lap time from 1:24.735 to 1:23.612 on screen, which is the argument for the whole architecture in one action.

Purple Stint

F1 Timing & Telemetry Analysis · Next.js · WebGL
Next.jsTypeScriptWebGLreact-three-fiberEChartsTelemetryData PipelineStatic ExportDesign SystemsTesting

The tool. Purple Stint takes two seasons of Formula 1 timing and telemetry, around 200 sessions, and publishes them as a static site: every page pre-rendered at build time, no API behind it, no runtime network calls. Each session gets nine views, from a results table through telemetry comparison to a 3D reconstruction of the lap. The analysis itself is written as plain TypeScript modules with no framework in them, so it is tested directly rather than through a browser: 270 unit tests against the real data on disk, and 39 Playwright tests against the built export, which is what a host would actually serve.

Placing a car from position, not distance. The obvious way to animate a lap is to drive each car along its recorded distance travelled. Doing that produced cars that stuttered and, at times, jumped. The cause turned out to be measurable: consecutive distance fixes imply speeds ranging from 9 to 5 533 km/h, because the channel is accumulated and its errors accumulate with it. Rebuilding the animation from the position channel instead places every car within about 4 m of where it actually was, which is narrower than the track. The lap is then resampled with a clamped cubic Hermite so the pace is right at every knot and continuous in between: only about 45 samples survive decimation, and straight lines between them held a car at one average speed for two seconds at a time, up to 43 km/h away from its real pace.

Reading a race, not a results table. The progression view draws the race trace, every driver's gap to a selectable reference lap by lap, with pit stops, safety cars and virtual safety cars marked in place, so an undercut shows up as what it is: a gap that closes across two laps rather than a number in a column. The strategy view is a tyre-stint Gantt built as a custom ECharts series, with compound, stint length and tyre age per driver. The undercut view goes further and detects the pit battles automatically, pairing drivers whose gap swings across a stop and charting each one.

Time in the pit lane is not time stopped. A pit stop as usually reported is the pit lane delta, entry to exit, which folds in the approach, the speed limit and the exit, and is mostly a property of the circuit. What a viewer wants is the time the car was stationary with the wheels off. That is not in the data, but it is recoverable from it: the out-lap speed trace runs to zero while the car is on the jacks, and the length of that zero is the stop. Applied across both seasons it resolves 873 of 1 216 stops, with a median of 2.56 s and a 5th percentile of 1.80 s. The 1.80 is the check that the method is sound rather than merely plausible: it is the world record, so the floor of the distribution lands exactly where physics puts it.

Everything in the scene is scaled from the data. The circuit outline is fitted into a fixed box, so a world unit is 27 m at Jeddah and 10 m at Monaco, and every constant tuned by eye was right for exactly one circuit. The scene therefore derives its sizes rather than setting them: the road is a fixed share of the frame, and the car's width is derived from the road's, so about seven and a half cars fit across the track, as they do in reality. That relation cannot drift when someone adjusts the other, which it had twice, once to a car three times the width of its own lane and once to a car a third of what it should be, and neither is visible until you count. Measured off the rendered pixels afterwards: 404 px of road, 53 px of car, 7.6 abreast. Five cameras ride with the car. The onboard is placed off the model rather than by eye, on its own roll hoop, and is the one view with its own field of view: from there the front wheels fall 25° below the lens, and at the scene's 34° a tilt steep enough to catch them would be aimed at the tarmac. It runs at 62° instead, which is why a broadcast onboard is a wide lens. The car is a CC BY 4.0 model decimated from 267 632 to 14 019 triangles and baked into a 300 KiB binary, repainted per team from an instance colour buffer. The whole scene, road, kerbs, six cars and six telemetry ribbons, is six draw calls.

Standings derived, not fetched. Both championships are computed from the classifications on disk rather than taken from a standings API, which means they can be checked: across the 49 scoring sessions of a season the points awarded come to exactly 101 per race and 36 per sprint, and the win counts equal the calendar. The season page also carries the round-by-round history behind the table: points per round, finishing and qualifying positions, the championship bump chart, and every team-mate head-to-head. The ideal lap view reconstructs each driver's theoretical best from their own fastest sectors.

The data stops where the season does. A telemetry export only holds weekends that have been run, and carries one date per event, the Sunday, recovered from the first race-control message. A calendar built on it alone ends at the last race, which on a season in progress reads as a season that finishes in September. So the fixture list comes from a second source and the two are merged: the calendar says what the rounds are and when each session starts, the export says what happened at the ones already run. A round is done only when the data has a race for it, and only then does it carry a winner, a pole or a link into its telemetry. Each card draws its circuit as a single path resampled from the same outline the 3D scene is built from. That second source is CC BY-NC-SA, non-commercial with a ShareAlike term, and it is the only such dependency in the project, so it is deliberately quarantined: one script writes it, one module reads it, and deleting its two files makes the page fall back to the rounds on disk without touching anything else. A test asserts that fallback, including that no session then claims a start time it cannot have.

Colour as data, not decoration. Every colour is a token in one stylesheet and no component may hold a literal, which a test enforces. Each of the eleven sections owns its own accent hue, and the hues are chosen by search rather than by taste: a sweep of the sRGB cube for the colour furthest in CIEDE2000 from all 53 already in use, meaning the other accents, the timing and compound semantics, and the 21 team colours the data carries. The most recent one came out at 6.03:1 contrast and ΔE00 17.35 from its nearest neighbour, against a spec minimum of 15. Team colours are reserved for identity and never reused as interface colour, because a series colour on a chart is data. The whole thing exports to 8 220 files at 41 % of Cloudflare Pages' file cap, with a median page of 130 KB and no page over 0.4 MB, and the build fails if either limit is crossed.

What it is not. The site is unofficial and non-commercial, not associated with the Formula 1 companies, and carries that notice on every page. The calendar source is CC BY-NC-SA and the only non-permissive dependency in the project, which is why it is isolated and why the repository's NOTICE.md says so plainly. One browser test is flaky, a draw-call assertion that fails roughly one run in three and passes on re-run, known and not yet fixed. The live-timing half of the project is not in the public repository and never will be, because it reads F1's own feed with a personal subscription.

The whole thing, in one pass. A recorded walkthrough of the site as it is used: a session opened and compared lap by lap, the race read through its progression and strategy, the circuit driven from three of its five cameras, and then the season — standings, calendar and the globe. Rendered from the static export, so it is the site itself rather than a mock-up.

Model-Based Torque Vectoring

4WD Electric Vehicle · CAT16x · BCNeMotorsport
MATLABSimulinkVI-GradeQP OptimizationDual-Track NLTV ModelPacejkaROSCAN Bus

Torque Vectoring on a 4WD vehicle with one independent motor per wheel is one of the most complete chassis control problems in motorsport. The system has three layers running in sequence: a nonlinear vehicle model that represents what the car is doing, a yaw rate controller that decides how much corrective yaw moment to apply, and a torque allocator that distributes that moment across all four motors in real time. Each layer feeds the next, and the whole chain runs in the processing unit of the CAT16x during competition.

The vehicle model is a Dual-Track Nonlinear Time Varying (NLTV) formulation. Unlike a simple bicycle model, it tracks all four wheels individually, accounting for lateral and longitudinal load transfer, aerodynamic downforce, Ackermann steering geometry, and a Pacejka Magic Formula tire model per wheel. Parameters vary nonlinearly with vertical load and slip angle, making it accurate both in the linear and limit handling regimes. The model was calibrated against real CAT15x telemetry from three maneuvers: Ramp Steer (96.7% fit), Skid Pad (98.8% fit), and the AutoX at Formula Student Spain 2023 (82.5% fit).

The yaw rate controller generates the reference corrective yaw moment that the torque allocator then executes. It combines two components: a Feed-Forward path that uses the vehicle model to anticipate the required moment based on steering input and speed, and a PID Feed-Back path that closes the loop on the measured yaw rate error. An anti-windup mechanism (back-calculation) prevents integrator saturation in transients, and a velocity-dependent reset avoids excessive integral buildup. The combined controller tracks the yaw rate reference with average errors of 0.02% in Ramp Steer, 0.18% in Skid Pad, and 0.89% in AutoX conditions.

The torque allocator translates the desired longitudinal force (from the driver throttle) and corrective yaw moment (from the controller) into four individual motor torque commands. This is posed as a multi-objective quadratic programming problem solved in real time. The optimizer respects motor torque limits, inverter limits, and the tire friction circle per wheel, while simultaneously minimizing the error on both references. Using Ackermann-corrected wheel angles, it distributes torque asymmetrically between left-right and front-rear to generate the exact yaw moment needed without sacrificing traction. Closed-loop tracking errors: Fx average 0.9%, Mz average 3.6%.

The entire system was developed and tested in a Simulink and VI-Grade CarRealTime loop before deployment on the real car. VI-Grade's 14-DOF multibody model replicated the CAT15x dynamics with full suspension, tire-road interaction, and real mass distribution. Three standard maneuvers were used to stress-test each layer independently and combined. Peak lateral acceleration in the AutoX improved by +7.9% over the baseline. On the hardware side, the algorithm runs as a ROS node on the car's Processing Unit, receiving data over CAN from the VectorNav INS, wheel resolvers and suspension sensors, and sending torque commands to two Lenze inverters at real-time rate.

A key design decision was making the system fully configurable in real time. Three driving modes were defined: Eco, Sport, and Hardcore. Each mode adjusts the aggressiveness of the yaw rate target and the weighting of the QP objectives, letting the driver tune the car behavior from a dashboard switch without stopping. The controller was deployed and validated on the CAT16x for the 2023-2024 competition season.

Traction Control

Wheel slip management · CAT15x/16x
Inverter ControlSlip RatioPacejkaCAN BusROS

The traction control system is implemented as a speed controller running directly inside the inverter, rather than at the Processing Unit level. This is a deliberate architectural choice: the CAN-ROS communication link between the PU and the inverters runs at 50 Hz, which is too slow to react to wheel slip transients effectively. By pushing the control loop inside the inverter, which runs at a significantly higher frequency, the system responds to slip events before they can develop, without being bottlenecked by communication latency.

Beyond torque limiting, the system also enforces a velocity-based control using an optimal slip ratio target per wheel. Since competition surfaces are known and consistent, characterized during pre-season testing, the optimal operating point on the longitudinal force curve is determined in advance. The team modeled the Pacejka Magic Formula for their specific tires, giving a much more accurate peak slip ratio prediction than a generic model. The optimal slip ratio target is then adjusted as a function of slip angle per wheel, coupling the longitudinal and lateral tire behavior for a more complete slip management strategy.

Regenerative Braking

Energy recovery · CAT15x/16x
Energy RecoveryBrake BlendingAnti-LockupSoC ManagementHydraulic SensorsROS

The regenerative braking system is designed around a single non-negotiable constraint: the ideal braking force distribution curve must always be maintained. Every mode and configuration operates within this envelope, ensuring vehicle stability and predictable brake bias regardless of how the electrical and mechanical forces are combined.

In performance-oriented scenarios such as AutoX, mechanical and electrical braking work in parallel from the first brake input. Hydraulic pressure sensors on both brake circuits measure the driver's braking demand in real time, allowing the system to calculate the current mechanical braking torque. Electrical braking torque is then adjusted to complement it precisely, giving the driver full pedal sensitivity while maximizing deceleration. The combined force distribution stays on the ideal curve throughout the braking event.

When energy recovery is the priority, the system gives the driver an initial pedal travel handled entirely by the electric motors. Only when the electrical braking force is insufficient do the mechanical brakes engage, and both systems work in parallel again. This staging maximizes energy sent back to the battery on circuits where recovery matters more than peak deceleration, without the driver needing to change their braking technique.

The system includes a wheel lockup prevention mechanism that compares individual wheel RPMs against the vehicle speed from the INS. If any wheel decelerates faster than physically consistent with the vehicle speed, electrical torque is reduced on that corner. A set of safety limits also monitors battery State of Charge and motor and inverter temperatures. If any threshold is exceeded, regenerative torque is progressively reduced with a smooth transition to pure mechanical braking so the driver is never caught off guard.

UNI — 02

TrustDrive / Aura

Advanced Human Machine Interfaces · Politecnico di Milano · 2026
HMI DesignAutonomous DrivingExplainable AIReact · Python · FastAPIPolitecnico di Milano · 2026

TrustDrive / Aura is a research prototype exploring how adaptive HMI design can support trust calibration in SAE Level 3 autonomous vehicles. Developed as a group capstone for the Advanced Human Machine Interfaces course at Politecnico di Milano, the project addresses a structural problem inherent to L3 automation: while the vehicle handles all lateral and longitudinal control, the driver must remain ready to retake control on demand. This creates the Out-of-the-Loop (OOTL) syndrome, where cognitive disengagement from the driving task makes safe handover costly in situational awareness and reaction time. The central design question was how to build trust gradually, so that drivers feel genuinely confident relying on the system over time rather than just tolerating it.

The answer is Aura, a personalized, voice-driven in-car interface that models each driver across five behavioral dimensions and adapts its communication style, timing, and information density accordingly. The interaction covers the full journey lifecycle: an indirect onboarding interview establishes a driver profile before the first drive; a proactive suggestion overlay invites highway automation with personalized benefit messaging; voice commands allow real-time queries during the automated phase; a three-stage Takeover Request (TOR) sequence reconstructs situational awareness progressively rather than through a sudden alert; and a post-drive feedback screen refines the profile over repeated sessions. Every system decision is surfaced with an explanation, applying Explainable AI (XAI) principles to help each driver build an accurate mental model of what the system can and cannot do.

The prototype runs as a full-stack web application: a React 19 + TanStack Start frontend serves as the in-car display, connected via WebSocket to a Python/FastAPI backend that manages speech-to-text (Whisper), LLM-based intent classification (Groq Llama 3.3 70B), and text-to-speech (Microsoft Edge TTS). The driver profile is stored as a five-dimension JSON object on a continuous [0, 10] scale and visualized as a pentagon radar chart accessible from the home screen.
