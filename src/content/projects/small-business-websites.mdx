**The idea.** A small restaurant, a guesthouse, a garage or a florist spends a good part of the day answering the same phone questions: are you open, is there a room that weekend, how much will it cost, how is my car, can it arrive today. These nine sites are demos for a web studio aimed at those businesses, three for each sector, and each one is built around the question its owner answers most. The answer is the first thing on the page, and it is interactive: the visitor chooses, the site calculates, and the result is on screen before anyone picks up a phone.

**What they share.** Every business is invented, with its own place, people and history, and every site says so. Each one has a visual world of its own, chosen among four built prototypes, so that no two look alike. They are static Astro sites with no third-party requests, no cookies and self-hosted fonts. Prices are always visible, bookings, orders and requests are simulated and say so, and nothing is presented as a review, an award or a real fact that is not one. Illustrations that look painted are made in code: authored SVG passed through a procedural gouache and paper filter in Python, since no image model was used. Photographs are stock with their licence recorded.

**Restaurants.** A neighbourhood bar, a mountain inn that lives on weekend bookings and a tasting menu do not need the same website. The three restaurant sites cover three ways of receiving a guest.

**Can Sifó, a vermouth bar in Sants, Barcelona.** A family bar since 1962, with the same marble counter, a vermouth barrel and a soda siphon on every table. The phone kept ringing on Mondays, the day it closes, a group of ten would turn up on Saturday without warning, and a coeliac neighbour asked dish by dish what she could eat. The site answers before anyone leaves home. A lit sign says whether the bar is open and when it closes, from the opening hours and Barcelona time. A gilded mirror shows the dish of the day. The menu filters by vegetarian, gluten free and the 14 allergens, hiding what cannot be eaten and saying how many dishes it hid. Groups write how many they are and when, and the message is ready to send by WhatsApp. Catalan and Spanish.

**Fonda Solpost, a mountain inn with its own booking system.** A village inn on a valley road, open since 1974, with a charcoal grill and spoon dishes. Saturdays used to depend on a notebook by the till and a phone ringing in the middle of service, and the guests who could not get through booked on a platform that charged for every diner. This is the complete one: a public site in four languages and a staff dashboard. A visitor sees tonight's free times, picks one and gets a booking code and a confirmation email. The booking engine seats it at the smallest free table that fits, joining up to three tables of the same room when needed, never the round ones. In the dashboard the new booking appears on the floor plan, reminders go out with one click, walk-ins and phone bookings are added the same way, and a waiting list keeps the guests who found no table.

**Fonoll, a tasting menu in a farmhouse in the Empordà.** A chef who turned the family cellar into a dining room with a brick vault and one south window. Guests kept asking whether lunch or dinner was better, and the answer was two different lights. The home page draws the room in section: lunch lets in the sun at the real angle for that day and place at 13:30, dinner turns the room to evening and lights the lamps. The menu runs step by step with the time each course arrives and the total beside it, wine pairing included. The booking form takes the 14 allergens and a note, so the kitchen knows before service, and a gift voucher draws itself as it is filled in. Catalan and Spanish.

**Accommodation.** Whoever looks for a place to stay compares it with the big platforms. A site of its own only wins if it shows at once what the platform shows badly, and if booking direct pays off. Each of these three answers a different question: are there nights left and what will the whole stay cost, which room do I want, and how far is the lift and in which language will they tell me.

**Cal Peirot, a guesthouse in the Cerdanya.** A stone house from 1884 whose grandmother, when it started letting rooms, kept a jar on a shelf for every night: full and lidded if taken, empty if free. The site is that shelf. Every night of the next two months is a jar, for each room or for the whole house, and changing room fills and empties them in a wave. A brown paper tag beside it adds the nights by season, the cleaning and the tourist tax into one total, turns over to show what is included, and warns before anything is sent when a bank holiday needs three nights. The request form arrives already filled with the stay. Catalan and Spanish, over several pages for the house, the valley and the way there.

**Casa Serinyana, a boutique hotel on the Onyar in Girona.** Eleven rooms in a merchant's house of the old town, each with a window of its own, and one with a painted ceiling found under a false one. On the platforms it looked like any other hotel. Here the facade is the room picker: each window is one of four room types with its price, and choosing one opens the shutters, lights the window and lights its reflection in the river. The panel beside it prices the chosen nights, with weekends dearer, and shows what booking direct saves on breakfast, a figure that returns next to the four reasons to book here.

**Coll d'Isard, ski apartments in Soldeu, Andorra.** Eight apartments 120 m from the gondola, and the same two questions every day in three languages: how far is the slope, and how much is our week. The home page is a ski poster painted in gouache that answers the first. Under it, the booking strip takes the dates, the people and the parking and prices the exact week by season, with the discount for seven nights. Three tickets say which apartment fits and whether it is free, and when one is taken the site moves to the next. Catalan, Spanish and French.

**Shops and services.** A shop, a garage or an office does not sell an experience. It answers one concrete, repeated question, every day, by phone. The site serves them if it answers that question before the customer has to call: what this bouquet costs and whether it can arrive today, how my car is doing and what it will cost, which tax deadline is next and what to bring.

**Floristeria Ginesta, a florist in Gràcia, Barcelona.** A florist who buys at the flower market at six and makes six bouquets every Monday, then spends midday answering the same messages. The site is a Saint George's Day stall all year round. The six bouquets are painted on a wooden counter, each with a handwritten card of its three prices, and touching a price circles it in red pen. A paper tag says whether an order still arrives today and how long is left. The order sheet beside it takes pickup or delivery with the cost for each neighbourhood, the day and the card message, and writes the WhatsApp message as it is filled in. Condolences sit apart, in white and green, with no ribbon.

**Tallers Gurri, a garage on an industrial estate in Vic.** Three mechanics who once counted 31 calls in one day from customers asking how their car was doing. Everything here is one carbon-copy work order. Typing the number on the receipt fills the order and stamps each step done, received, diagnosis, waiting for a part, in repair, ready, with the date and the mechanic's handwritten note. The yellow copy is the estimate: a service and a vehicle type give the price range and what it includes. The pink copy is the diary: circle a free slot next week, add a courtesy car, and the appointment is summed up on the same sheet. The complete site of the sector, in Catalan and Spanish.

**Gestoria Pallarès, a tax and admin office in Sabadell.** Four people who explain the VAT return as the VAT you charged minus the VAT you paid, and clients who are not afraid of paying but of being late. The site is the office diary, open at the week of the next deadline with the last day circled in red and the days left in large figures. Choosing self-employed, small company or private turns the page to that person's deadline, and the facing page lists what to bring and the monthly fee. The calendar crosses out past deadlines by hand, every tax form is explained in plain words, and the first consultation asks for the reason so the right person answers. Dates come with a clear note that they are indicative and not advice.
