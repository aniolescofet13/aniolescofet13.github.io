A Python lap time simulation and vehicle analysis platform designed to support a racing car's full development cycle, from early concept where almost no hardware data exists, all the way through to race-ready validation against real on-track telemetry. The tool is built around the principle that the fidelity of the simulation should match the fidelity of the information available: as the team learns more about the car, the models are upgraded in place without restructuring the workflow.

**The problem it solves.** In motorsport development, the decisions with the biggest impact on performance — suspension geometry, aero package, powertrain architecture — are made at the very beginning of a project, when the least data is available. A tool that only works once full hardware data exists arrives too late. Equally, a tool that is too simplified fails to give useful answers once real data starts coming in. This simulator solves that by being continuously useful at every stage of development, from back-of-envelope concept studies to lap-accurate simulation validated against telemetry.

**Modular fidelity architecture.** Every subsystem carries its own ladder of physical fidelity, selectable at runtime, and the ladders are deliberately different lengths: each one stops where the extra physics stops paying for itself. L1 is the concept stage, simple scalar parameters such as constant peak power, fixed drag and lift coefficients and constant friction, enough for a credible lap time from a one-page spec sheet. L2 is development, with physics-based models: torque curves, brake bias and hydraulic geometry, load-sensitive tyre behaviour, lateral load transfer. Brakes finish here, at the full hydraulic model. L3 is validation, where Pacejka Magic Formula tyres fitted to measured data arrive with thermal, wear and pressure on top of them, and the chassis gains pitch compliance. L4 is the detail stage, and only three subsystems have one: Powertrain for ERS energy management, Aerodynamics for the ground-effect and ride-height map, and Suspension for roll centres. Levels mix freely, so Tyres L3 can run beside Suspension L1 when that is all the data supports, which leaves 4 × 4 × 4 × 3 × 3 × 2 = **1,152 model combinations** behind a single call signature. The solver never knows which level is active: every subsystem exposes the same interface, and higher-fidelity models are drop-in replacements.

**Layer 1: QSS spatial sweep.** The spatial engine is built on the Quasi-Steady-State method, the industry standard in motorsport simulation. The car is treated as a point mass navigating a known racing line, assumed to be in steady state at every spatial point. The algorithm runs in three linked sweeps across the track distance domain. First, corner speed limits: at every point with curvature κ, the maximum lateral speed is solved iteratively, since downforce and therefore grip depend on speed itself. Second, a forward acceleration sweep propagates the speed envelope accounting for engine power, drag, rolling resistance, and remaining longitudinal grip after cornering demand is subtracted via the friction ellipse. Third, a backward braking sweep runs the same logic in reverse, propagating maximum entry speeds into each corner. Both sweeps wrap around the lap, up to five times, until they stop changing, so the lap starts at the speed it ends; the lower of the two profiles is the minimum-time speed envelope: at every track point the car is at exactly one limit — traction, braking, or cornering.

**Layer 2: transient temporal integration and iterative coupling.** Once the spatial speed profile is converged, a second pass integrates the trajectory in the time domain, computing the states that evolve continuously along the lap. The tyre thermal model tracks compound temperature using a lumped thermal network, updating friction coefficients and slip stiffness that feed back into Layer 1. Suspension and chassis dynamics compute instantaneous ride height as a function of heave and roll, which directly modifies the aero maps used in the spatial sweep. ERS state of charge is integrated from the power demand profile, determining deployment availability corner by corner. Fuel mass drops continuously, shifting total weight and CG height. These outputs are fed back into Layer 1, which re-solves the speed profile with the updated vehicle state. The two layers iterate to convergence, typically in five to fifteen passes under an adaptive relaxation, producing a speed profile that accounts for both instantaneous physics and the evolving state of the car across the full lap.

**Sensitivity analysis and signal explorer.** Rather than running the simulator once per configuration, the sensitivity module sweeps a parameter over a range and plots the lap time response directly, answering questions like: at what downforce level does added drag outweigh added cornering grip, what is the weight penalty per second on a technical versus a high-speed circuit, or at what CG height does lateral load transfer start to hurt more than it helps. The signal explorer provides a graphical interface to inspect every computed channel as a function of track position or time — speed, longitudinal and lateral acceleration, tyre load per axle, aerodynamic downforce, brake force distribution, engine operating point, ERS contribution — and overlays channels from different configurations to directly show where one setup gains on another. The GG diagram is included as a standard assessment of how efficiently the tyre friction circle is being used.

**Real data validation loop.** Once the car is running on track, the simulator's outputs are compared directly against real telemetry — GPS speed, IMU accelerations, wheel speeds, brake pressure, engine channels — aligned spatially by arc length. Systematic offsets in specific sectors reveal which subsystem model needs refinement: a braking point consistently too early points to the brake model, a corner speed consistently too high points to the lateral tyre model. This closes the loop between simulation and reality and gives each model upgrade a clear validation target.

**Checked on a car I know.** The Formula Student Electric preset is my former team's car, built from the team's own data and checked, not tuned, against its four autocross runs at Formula Student Germany 2026. The first question is whether the model is right. Shadow mode imposes each run's logged line and speed, and the model derives everything else: lateral and longitudinal acceleration, motor speed and battery power follow the logs, battery power correlating at 0.98-0.99 on every run, and the net battery energy of the two clean runs lands within 1-3 %. Getting there was the useful part, because each fix was a measurement rather than a fudge: a track smoothing sized for Grand Prix circuits that flattened the slalom, a tyre scaling factor left out of the fit, power channels that missed the tyre scrub the solver already charged, and a fixed draw of a few kilowatts, the low-voltage system through the DC-DC, pumps and the inverters' fixed losses, on top of the drive chain's losses. The team's data stays private; the figures show the comparison, not the car's parameters.

**What the driver left on the table.** With the model trusted, the solver drives the same line itself, from a standing start and following no speed profile, as fast as grip, power and brakes allow: the time a flawless driver would have set on the line that was actually driven. On the best run that is 83.7 s against the 85.4 s driven; the time trace and the map show where along the course it opens.

**And on a car everyone knows.** The Formula 1 preset is checked against one car only, Verstappen's 2026 Suzuka qualifying lap (1:30.262) from the public timing data, because no two Formula 1 cars are alike. The same two questions, in the same order. With his speed imposed, the engine speed follows the log within 34 rpm on average and the gear matches on 84 % of the lap, once the gear ratios were measured on that lap and the engine curve shaped by the fuel-flow limits of the 2026 power-unit rules. Lateral acceleration correlates at 0.95 with an estimate from the car's position, and the brake pedal agrees with the model on 94 % of the lap. Below 300 km/h the power the speed trace needs stays under the model's engine-plus-ERS ceiling, and on a third of the time he is flat out it is under what the engine alone gives: the 2026 car recharging its battery at full throttle.

**How close the lap gets.** Driving the lap itself, on the circuit's reference racing line, the model sets 1:29.962, 0.30 s under Verstappen. The sectors are further apart than the lap, and the figures show why: the model spends its electrical energy as fast as the rules allow from the first straight, where a real car saves it for the back straight, 288 km/h there against his 328, and through the esses it has less grip than he uses. An energy-deployment strategy and the medium-speed grip are the next model changes.

**The application.** The simulator runs as a FastAPI service behind a React front end, across its screens: a garage where the car is built subsystem by subsystem with live capability curves, a track browser, the lap and race runner, a signal explorer, a 3D replay, a parameter sweep streamed over a WebSocket, and telemetry validation. A qualifying lap solves in about 0.05 s, and the heaviest configuration, with every thermal, wear and energy feedback enabled, in about 6 s. That is down from 66 s before the interpolation tables were cached: profiling found the solver constructing 231,000 spline interpolators per lap.

**3D replay.** The solved lap is replayed in WebGL with the car posed from prebuilt interpolants, so a frame costs four table lookups rather than a search. Five cameras: chase, a chassis-mounted onboard, a planted trackside vantage, an overhead, and a free orbit that rides with the car. The onboard is the one worth explaining — it is parented into the car's own body group rather than having its pose recomputed each frame from the racing line's heading. Heading derived from a resampled line carries noise, and aiming a camera forty metres down the track multiplies a small angular error into a visibly shaking picture; bolting the camera to the car makes that class of error impossible, because any residual noise turns the camera and the bodywork together. The car is a scanned AMR25 reduced to 2.3 MB — Draco geometry, WebP textures — which loads in 14 ms and keeps its wheels as separate nodes so they turn; the whole replay route is lazy-loaded, because three.js has no business in the bundle of a screen that never draws it.

**How far the models go.** Suspension carries a fourth level that splits lateral load transfer into its geometric and elastic paths through the roll centres, because routing every newton through the springs and dividing by roll stiffness alone cannot express two cars with identical roll rates and different roll-centre heights. Tyre inflation pressure is an opt-in feedback, modelled as a window either side of an optimum rather than a slope, since a linear term would reward ever-lower pressure. Drive torque distribution across an axle is explicit: evaluating the friction ellipse on the whole axle load only holds if torque can follow load, so the parameter adds no torque-vectoring bonus, it charges for its absence, with an open differential costing 1.3 s a lap at Catalunya. Formula Student has its own dynamic events, because every circuit in the database is a Grand Prix track and an FS car simulated on one spends 86 % of the lap above 120 km/h, a regime the class never reaches.
